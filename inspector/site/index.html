whitakerLib

A place to read Latin. Click a word to see what it could mean and what forms it could take. Once the dictionary has loaded, you can keep reading offline in this tab.

Start with the opening of Caesar’s Gallic War, bring your own Latin, or pick one of 102 books. Your texts and reading positions are remembered, so you can return to where you left off. What you read and look up stays in your browser: only a book you fetch comes from Perseus.

The same dictionary runs on a Kindle from 2015. With the KOReader plugin, you can take it with you and read Latin offline. With the Firefox extension, you can look up words on other websites without leaving the page, even if you’ve gone offline!

Underneath all three is a small C++ library built from William Whitaker’s WORDS. Its C and Python bindings are generated from the C++ interface using reflection, so all three are easy to keep in sync. They’re there if you’d like to build something of your own.

It took 102 Latin books and 4.66 million word lookups to match WORDS, with my fixes to it: every remaining difference is a guess left outside the library or one of three cases where WORDS lost its answer. Now I can start looking at where the readings themselves could be better. See the comparison.

It lists the readings WORDS’ dictionary gives a word, without choosing one for the sentence. That dictionary has gaps of its own: some words, names especially, are missing or incomplete there, so they are here too. The popup shows dictionary forms such as amo, amare, amavi, amatus.

Help

Reading the text

Click or tap a word to see its possible readings. Close the popup by tapping the word again, clicking outside it or pressing Esc. Explain this answer, at the bottom of every popup, says what each line means.

Use the left and right arrow keys to move between words, and the up and down arrow keys to move between lines.

Adding text

Type or paste Latin in Add Text and select “Read it” or press Ctrl+Enter. You can also paste Latin anywhere on the reading page to open it straight away.

To read a book, choose one from the list and select “Fetch”. Your browser downloads it from Perseus, so a new book requires an internet connection; once it is open, you can keep reading it offline in this tab.

Every text remembers its own reading position. You can build your own little library of Latin books and passages.

How it works

Rather than storing every form a Latin word can take, the dictionary stores its parts and the relationships between them.

Two tries walk the word from opposite ends, one looking for stems and the other for endings. Where they meet, the library knows which combinations Latin allows according to its dictionary data.

From 3.8 MB, it can produce over a million spellings and more than two million readings.

Following a lookup

Enter a word to follow the steps the library takes to read it. See what it tried, what it found, and how each reading came to be.

Choose “the whole text” to see where the library spends its time.

Looking at a whole text

How many words in the Aeneid could be verbs? How many could be nouns? How long does it take to find out?

Statistics looks at every reading the library returns for every word in your text, and measures how long the library takes to find them.

A word can count as both a noun and a verb. These are possibilities, not decisions about what the author meant.

Using the library

See a word go through the library’s API: the calls you would make in C++, C or Python, and what each one returns. Choose the language and which functions to show.

Settings

Choose a theme or follow your system settings.

Developer options contain things I’m testing to see how they might fit into the reader. They may be incomplete, wrong, or disappear altogether.

Moving around

Press Space to open Your Library. Use the arrow keys to select a book or passage and Enter to pick up where you left off.

Press x for the text, p for API, r for Trace, t for Statistics, i to add text, n for Info, g for Settings and ? for Help.
